## Standard 1: Nature of Science — The Practice of Science (SC.3.N.1)

**Strand:** Nature of Science
**Big Idea Group:** The Practice of Science
**Purpose:** Students engage in authentic scientific inquiry — raising questions, investigating, recording data, communicating findings, and understanding the nature of scientific evidence. This is the foundation for all science learning.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.1

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.1
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Raise questions about the natural world, investigate them individually and in teams through free exploration and systematic investigations, and generate appropriate explanations based on those explorations.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students practice the full inquiry cycle — from generating a question to designing and carrying out investigations (both open/exploratory and structured), to communicating what they found. Both individual and collaborative inquiry are expected.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Assuming all science follows a single rigid "scientific method" in a fixed sequence; confusing observations with explanations/inferences.
- **Glossary terms:** Empirical evidence, investigation, hypothesis, explanation, inference.
- **Vertical alignment:** Builds toward SC.4.N.1.1 (more rigorous inquiry using reference materials and systematic methods).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - STEM Lab Grade 3 (5020100)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.1.In.1 — Ask questions, explore, observe, and identify outcomes.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.1.Su.1 — Ask literal questions, explore, observe, and share information.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.1.Pa.1 — Explore, observe, and recognize common objects in the natural world.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1626)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner frame a testable question, describe an investigation plan, and explain a conclusion based on their findings?
  - Misconception to detect: Writing a conclusion that goes beyond what the evidence shows; confusing "what I observed" with "what I think happened."
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story arc where a character encounters a natural mystery (why do apples brown? why do some areas stay cooler?). The learner is given simulated investigation data and asked to draw a conclusion — or is asked to design a follow-up question.
  - Learner evidence: Frames a question in testable form; references specific observations as evidence for their explanation.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.2

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.2
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Compare the observations made by different groups using the same tools and seek reasons to explain the differences across groups.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students recognize that even using the same tools, different investigators can get different results — and that this is a normal part of science requiring explanation, not just error correction. Builds measurement accuracy and scientific communication skills.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Assuming different results mean one group is "wrong"; not recognizing that natural variability, measurement technique, and tool precision all affect outcomes.
- **Glossary terms:** Observation, comparison, variability, measurement.
- **Vertical alignment:** Leads to SC.4.N.1.2 (comparing observations using multiple tools).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.1.In.2 — Work with a group to make observations and identify results.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.1.Su.2 — Work with a partner to make observations.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.1.Pa.2 — Assist with investigations with a partner.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1627)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify that two groups got different measurements and propose a plausible reason for the difference?
  - Misconception to detect: Dismissing one group's results as simply "wrong" without offering a reason tied to measurement, technique, or natural variation.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story where two characters in different parts of the town measure the same thing (e.g., temperature, rainfall, number of insects) and get different numbers. The learner must explain why.
  - Learner evidence: Names at least one reason the results differ that is grounded in the investigation setup (e.g., "they used a smaller cup," "their thermometer was in the sun").

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.3

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.3
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Keep records as appropriate, such as pictorial, written, or simple charts and graphs, of investigations conducted.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students select an appropriate recording format for their investigation type. Pictorial records for qualitative observations, written notes for descriptions, charts/tables for systematic comparisons, simple graphs for data with quantity.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Recording only conclusions rather than actual observations during the investigation; not distinguishing observations from inferences in notes.
- **Glossary terms:** Data, record, chart, graph, observation.
- **Vertical alignment:** Leads to SC.4.N.1.3 (more advanced record-keeping and distinguishing observations from inferences).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.1.In.3 — Record observations to describe findings using written or visual formats, such as picture stories.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.1.Su.3 — Record observations to describe findings using dictated words and phrases and pictures.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.1.Pa.1 — Explore, observe, and recognize common objects in the natural world.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1628)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Given a completed investigation, can the learner select the most appropriate record format and explain why it fits the data?
  - Misconception to detect: Choosing a graph for data that has no numerical values; using a picture when quantitative comparison is needed.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer investigator character who kept messy notes on an experiment; the learner helps reorganize the findings into a better format and explains the choice.
  - Learner evidence: Selects the correct format (chart, graph, pictorial, or written) and provides a justification linked to the type of data collected.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.4

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.4
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize the importance of communication among scientists.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students understand that sharing findings, peer review, and open communication are what allow scientific knowledge to grow and be checked. Science is a collective, social endeavor — not a solo activity.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Believing that a single scientist's experiment is sufficient proof; not understanding that scientific claims need to be shared and checked by others.
- **Glossary terms:** Communication, peer review, scientific community.
- **Vertical alignment:** Builds on general inquiry norms; connects to N.1.5 (scientists check each other's work).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)

  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.1.Su.4 — Recognize that people work in different kinds of jobs related to science.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.1.Pa.3 — Recognize that people share information.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1629)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner explain why scientists share their findings and what happens when they don't?
  - Misconception to detect: Thinking a scientist's experiment is "done" once results are collected, with no need to share.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story where a character makes a discovery and must decide whether to share it with the community — the consequences of sharing vs. keeping it secret become the plot driver.
  - Learner evidence: Explains at least one benefit of scientists sharing results (e.g., others can check it, build on it, catch mistakes).

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.5

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.5
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize that scientists question, discuss, and check each other's evidence and explanations.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Peer review and critical evaluation of evidence are core scientific norms. Students learn that questioning another scientist's work is not rude — it's how science makes sure knowledge is reliable.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Believing that disagreeing with a scientist is disrespectful; thinking that once something is published it cannot be questioned.
- **Glossary terms:** Evidence, explanation, peer review, critique, validation.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends N.1.4 (communication) into active checking; leads to more nuanced scientific argumentation in later grades.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.1.In.4 — Recognize that scientists share their knowledge and results with each other.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1633)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify an example of scientists checking each other's work and explain why that's valuable?
  - Misconception to detect: Equating scientific questioning with personal attack; believing that a discovery is "proven" once one scientist reports it.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer scenario where two scientists disagree about what caused something in the natural world; the learner must evaluate both sides' evidence.
  - Learner evidence: Describes how checking others' work makes science more reliable; identifies which scientist's evidence is stronger in a scenario.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.6

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.6
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Infer based on observation.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students distinguish between what they directly observe and what they conclude from those observations. An inference is a logical interpretation of evidence — it goes beyond the observable fact but must be grounded in it.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Treating inferences as observations (claiming you "saw" something when you actually concluded it); making inferences that go beyond what the evidence supports.
- **Glossary terms:** Observation, inference, evidence, reasoning.
- **Vertical alignment:** Fundamental to all scientific reasoning; builds toward hypothesis formation and explanation-writing in later grades.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.1.In.1 — Ask questions, explore, observe, and identify outcomes.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1634)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Given a set of observations, can the learner state a logical inference and identify it as distinct from the observations?
  - Misconception to detect: Presenting an inference as a direct observation (e.g., "I saw that the plant was thirsty" vs. "The plant's leaves were drooping, so I think it needs water").
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer detective/investigator scene where evidence is found at a site (bent grass, muddy footprints, half-eaten food) and the learner must infer what happened without being told directly.
  - Learner evidence: States the observations separately from the inference; uses "I think" or "this suggests" language for the inference.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.7

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.7
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.1 — The Practice of Science
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Explain that empirical evidence is information, such as observations or measurements, that is used to help validate explanations of natural phenomena.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students understand that scientific explanations must be grounded in empirical evidence — data you can observe or measure. Opinion and tradition are not enough; science demands testable, observable support.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Believing that a "good story" or a popular belief counts as scientific evidence; not understanding that evidence must be observable/measurable to validate a scientific explanation.
- **Glossary terms:** Empirical evidence, validation, observation, measurement, natural phenomena.
- **Vertical alignment:** Builds toward understanding of scientific theories and the role of evidence in establishing scientific knowledge.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.1.In.1 — Ask questions, explore, observe, and identify outcomes.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1635)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify which of several statements qualifies as empirical evidence and explain why the others do not?
  - Misconception to detect: Accepting opinions, traditions, or unsupported claims as scientific evidence.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer town debate where multiple characters offer different "reasons" for why something happened in nature — some provide real observations and measurements, others provide opinions. The learner evaluates which are empirical.
  - Learner evidence: Correctly labels at least two examples as empirical or non-empirical and explains what makes each one qualify or not.

## Standard 2: Nature of Science — Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models (SC.3.N.3)

**Strand:** Nature of Science
**Big Idea Group:** The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models
**Purpose:** Students learn that science uses specific language and conceptual tools (models, laws, hypotheses) that have precise meanings different from everyday use. This standard develops scientific vocabulary and metacognition about how science works.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.3.1

- **Code:** SC.3.N.3.1
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.3 — The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize that words in science can have different or more specific meanings than their use in everyday language; for example, energy, cell, heat/cold, and evidence.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students actively compare the everyday meaning of a word with its scientific meaning. Examples provided: energy (everyday = "I have lots of energy today" vs. scientific = a measurable property of matter and systems), evidence (everyday = general proof vs. scientific = observable/measurable data).
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Assuming that if a word is familiar from everyday life, its scientific meaning is the same; using "energy" to mean enthusiasm in a science explanation.
- **Glossary terms:** Energy, cell, evidence, heat, model, matter — all terms with dual everyday/scientific meanings.
- **Vertical alignment:** Supports vocabulary precision across all science strands; especially important for P.10 (energy) and P.11 (heat).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - STEM Lab Grade 3 (5020100)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.3.In.1 — Recognize meanings of words used in science, such as energy, temperature, and gravity.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.3.Su.1 — Recognize meanings of words used in science, such as telescope, environment, and solid.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.3.Pa.1 — Recognize common objects related to science by name, such as ice, animal, and plant.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1636)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner distinguish the scientific definition of a term from its everyday meaning and use the scientific definition correctly in context?
  - Misconception to detect: Using "energy" to mean enthusiasm ("the plant had lots of energy") or "evidence" to mean any general statement.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer character gives a "report" that mixes everyday and scientific uses of the same words; the learner must flag the incorrect uses and rewrite the explanation.
  - Learner evidence: Correctly distinguishes everyday vs. scientific meaning for at least two terms; can use the scientific meaning in a sentence.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.3.2

- **Code:** SC.3.N.3.2
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.3 — The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize that scientists use models to help understand and explain how things work.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 1: Recall
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students identify examples of scientific models (a globe, a diagram, a classroom model of a solar system, a food web chart) and explain what the model helps them understand.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Thinking "model" means only a physical 3D object; not recognizing that diagrams, charts, and simulations are also models.
- **Glossary terms:** Model, representation, phenomenon.
- **Vertical alignment:** Connects to N.3.3 (limits of models); builds toward more complex model use in middle school.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.3.In.2 — Use models to identify how things work.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.3.Su.2 — Recognize that models represent real things.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.3.Pa.2 — Recognize a model of a real object.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1637)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify that a given representation is a model and explain what it helps us understand about the real thing?
  - Misconception to detect: Thinking a diagram of a plant is the same as the plant itself; not recognizing a food web chart as a model.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer character builds a model of something in their town (a water cycle, a solar system); the learner uses the model to answer questions about the real phenomenon.
  - Learner evidence: Identifies the model type; explains one thing the model helps you understand that would be hard to see directly.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.3.3

- **Code:** SC.3.N.3.3
- **Strand:** Nature of Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.N.3 — The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize that all models are approximations of natural phenomena; as such, they do not perfectly account for all observations.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students learn the critical scientific thinking skill of recognizing what a model leaves out. Every model simplifies reality — the question is whether the simplification is useful or misleading for a given purpose.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Thinking that a model that doesn't include every detail is "wrong"; conversely, assuming a simple model is perfectly accurate.
- **Glossary terms:** Model, approximation, limitation, simplification.
- **Vertical alignment:** Directly extends N.3.2; develops critical evaluation skills needed for middle school science models.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.N.3.In.3 — Identify that models are representations of things found in the real world.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.N.3.Su.2 — Recognize that models represent real things.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.N.3.Pa.2 — Recognize a model of a real object.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1638)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Given a model, can the learner identify at least one thing the model does not show accurately and explain why that limitation matters?
  - Misconception to detect: Treating a 2D diagram of Earth as perfectly accurate (e.g., assuming countries are the exact sizes shown on a flat map).
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer character uses a globe to plan a trip and notices something that doesn't match reality. The learner explains what the model left out.
  - Learner evidence: Names one specific limitation of a given model; explains whether that limitation matters for the purpose the model is being used for.

## Standard 3: Earth & Space Science — Earth in Space and Time (SC.3.E.5)

**Strand:** Earth & Space Science
**Big Idea Group:** Earth in Space and Time
**Purpose:** Students explore the Sun as a star, the nature of other stars, gravity, and telescopes as tools. They build a framework for understanding Earth's place in space.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.E.5.1

- **Code:** SC.3.E.5.1
- **Strand:** Earth & Space Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.E.5 — Earth in Space and Time
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Explain that stars can be different; some are smaller, some are larger, and some appear brighter than others; all except the Sun are so far away that they look like points of light.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students distinguish apparent brightness from actual size/distance. A star that looks bright might be large, close, or both. All stars except the Sun appear as points because of their immense distance.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Believing that brighter-looking stars are always larger; thinking all stars are the same; confusing planets (which don't twinkle) with stars.
- **Glossary terms:** Star, luminosity, apparent brightness, light year, point of light.
- **Vertical alignment:** Builds toward SC.5.E.5 (properties of the solar system and beyond).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.E.5.In.1 — Recognize that stars in the sky look different from each other.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.E.5.Su.1 — Recognize that all stars except the Sun appear very small.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.E.5.Pa.1 — Recognize stars in the sky.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1639)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner explain why stars look like tiny points of light even though some are much larger than the Sun?
  - Misconception to detect: Concluding that a dimmer star must be smaller; assuming all stars are the same size.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer night scene where characters are stargazing and debating which star is "biggest." The learner must explain why you can't judge size just by looking.
  - Learner evidence: Correctly explains that apparent brightness/size depends on both actual size and distance; states that all non-Sun stars are too far away to see as anything but points.

### Benchmark: SC.3.E.5.2

- **Code:** SC.3.E.5.2
- **Strand:** Earth & Space Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.E.5 — Earth in Space and Time
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Identify the Sun as a star that emits energy; some of it in the form of light.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students understand that the Sun is not fundamentally different from other stars — it is the same type of object, just much closer. It produces energy (electromagnetic radiation) and some of that energy reaches Earth as visible light and heat.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Thinking the Sun is a different type of object from stars; not recognizing that sunlight is a form of energy.
- **Glossary terms:** Star, energy, light, radiation, solar energy.
- **Vertical alignment:** Directly links to E.6.1 (Sun's energy heats Earth) and P.10 (forms of energy including light and heat).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.E.5.In.2 — Recognize that the Sun is a star that gives off its own light.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.E.5.Su.2 — Recognize that the Sun gives off light.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.E.5.Pa.2 — Recognize that the Sun is bright.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1640)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner correctly identify the Sun as a star and name at least two forms of energy it emits?
  - Misconception to detect: Treating the Sun as fundamentally different from "real" stars; thinking the Sun only produces light, not heat.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story set in a solar-powered community where a character explains where the energy in solar panels comes from; the learner traces it back to the Sun as a star.
  - Learner evidence: States that the Sun is a star; identifies light and heat as forms of energy the Sun emits; connects this to E.6.1 (Sun heating objects on Earth).

### Benchmark: SC.3.E.5.3

- **Code:** SC.3.E.5.3
- **Strand:** Earth & Space Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.E.5 — Earth in Space and Time
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize that the Sun appears large and bright because it is the closest star to Earth.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students apply reasoning about distance and apparent size: proximity, not actual size, explains why the Sun appears so large compared to other stars. This connects to E.5.1 (points of light).
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Believing the Sun is actually much larger than all other stars because it looks bigger; not understanding that apparent size depends on distance.
- **Glossary terms:** Apparent size, distance, proximity.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends E.5.1; builds toward understanding of light-year distances and solar system scale.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.E.5.In.3 — Recognize that the Sun is the closest star to Earth.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.E.5.Su.3 — Recognize that the Sun is a star.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1641)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner explain why the Sun looks bigger than other stars even if some other stars are actually larger?
  - Misconception to detect: Concluding that the Sun must be the largest star in the universe because it looks the biggest from Earth.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer character shows two flashlights of the same brightness — one close, one far. The learner explains how this models why the Sun looks bigger than other stars.
  - Learner evidence: States that apparent size/brightness depends on distance; correctly applies this to explain the Sun's appearance vs. other stars.

### Benchmark: SC.3.E.5.4

- **Code:** SC.3.E.5.4
- **Strand:** Earth & Space Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.E.5 — Earth in Space and Time
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Explore the Law of Gravity by demonstrating that gravity is a force that can be overcome.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning

### Benchmark: SC.3.L.14.2

- **Code:** SC.3.L.14.2
- **Strand:** Life Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.L.14 — Organization and Development of Living Organisms
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Investigate and describe how plants respond to stimuli (heat, light, gravity), such as the way plant stems grow toward light and their roots grow downward in response to gravity.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students observe and explain three tropisms: phototropism (stems grow toward light), gravitropism (roots grow toward gravity/downward), thermotropism (some plants respond to heat). Key: explaining the "why" — these responses help the plant survive.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Thinking plants "want" to grow toward light as a conscious act rather than as a biological response to stimuli; not connecting the response to survival advantage.
- **Glossary terms:** Stimuli, response, phototropism, gravitropism, tropism, growth.

- **Related courses or access points:**

  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.L.14.Su.2 — Recognize that plants grow toward light and roots grow down in the soil.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.L.14.Pa.2 — Recognize that plants grow.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1658)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner explain why a plant grew in a specific direction, naming the stimulus and the survival benefit of the response?
  - Misconception to detect: Attributing purposeful "wanting" to plant growth directions; not recognizing roots growing downward as a gravity response.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story where a character is experimenting with plants in unusual conditions (growing sideways, in a dark room with one light source); the learner predicts what will happen and explains why.
  - Learner evidence: Names the stimulus (light, gravity, heat); describes the response; explains how the response helps the plant survive (roots find water; stems find sunlight for photosynthesis).

## Standard 10: Life Science — Diversity and Evolution of Living Organisms (SC.3.L.15)

**Strand:** Life Science
**Big Idea Group:** Diversity and Evolution of Living Organisms
**Purpose:** Students classify living things — animals by physical characteristics and behaviors, and plants by reproductive strategy and physical characteristics.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.L.15.1

- **Code:** SC.3.L.15.1
- **Strand:** Life Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.L.15 — Diversity and Evolution of Living Organisms
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Classify animals into major groups (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, arthropods, vertebrates and invertebrates, those having live births and those which lay eggs) according to their physical characteristics and behaviors.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students apply a classification system using observable physical traits and behaviors. Key groups: vertebrates (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish) and invertebrates (arthropods and others). Multiple classification frameworks are used simultaneously — the same animal can be classified in multiple ways.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Classifying all animals that live in water as "fish"; thinking that all eggs come from birds; not recognizing arthropods as a group.
- **Glossary terms:** Mammal, bird, reptile, amphibian, fish, arthropod, vertebrate, invertebrate, physical characteristics.
- **Vertical alignment:** Foundation for biological classification; leads to taxonomy and evolutionary relationships in middle school.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)

  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.L.15.In.1 — Classify animals by a similar physical characteristic, such as fur, feathers, and number of legs.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.L.15.Su.1 — Sort common animals by observable characteristics.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.L.15.Pa.1 — Match animals that are the same.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1659)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner correctly classify an unfamiliar animal into its major group using physical characteristics and behaviors, and explain the key feature that determined the classification?
  - Misconception to detect: Classifying a dolphin as a fish (it's a mammal — breathes air, live births, has hair); thinking all egg-laying animals are birds.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer nature preserve or science museum scenario where a new animal is discovered; the learner uses physical characteristics to classify it into the correct group.

- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1660)

  - Skill observed: Can the learner classify a plant as flowering or nonflowering, and seed-producing or spore-producing, using observable physical characteristics?
  - Misconception to detect: Thinking pine trees have no seeds because they don't have flowers; confusing mossy/fern-like appearance with low classification certainty.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer forest exploration where a character encounters several unfamiliar plants; the learner sorts them into groups using the classification framework.
  - Learner evidence: Correctly places at least three plants into the right categories; names the observable characteristic that determined each classification; distinguishes seeds from spores.

## Standard 11: Life Science — Interdependence (SC.3.L.17)

**Strand:** Life Science
**Big Idea Group:** Interdependence
**Purpose:** Students investigate how living things are connected to and dependent on their environment — specifically how plants and animals respond to seasonal change, and how plants produce their own food using environmental inputs.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.L.17.1

- **Code:** SC.3.L.17.1
- **Strand:** Life Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.L.17 — Interdependence
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Describe how animals and plants respond to changing seasons.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students observe and describe seasonal changes in the appearance and behavior of plants and animals: leaf color change/drop in autumn, animal hibernation/migration in winter, plant dormancy, spring blooming, summer growth. Florida context: seasonal changes are subtler — focus on behavior changes and observable signs.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Thinking plants and animals in Florida don't show seasonal changes because there's no snow; not recognizing migration and hibernation as seasonal responses.
- **Glossary terms:** Season, response, behavior, hibernation, migration, dormancy, adaptation.
- **Vertical alignment:** Connects to L.14.2 (plants respond to stimuli); leads toward more complex ecosystem and adaptation concepts.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.L.17.In.1 — Identify changes in the appearance of animals and plants throughout the year.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.L.17.Su.1 — Recognize that the appearance of some plants in the environment changes throughout the year.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.L.17.Pa.1 — Recognize clothing worn by humans in different weather (seasons).
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1650)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner describe at least two different ways that living things in a specific environment respond to seasonal changes, and explain what triggers those responses?
  - Misconception to detect: Believing that only plants or only animals respond to seasons; thinking Florida organisms don't have seasonal responses.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story where a character's town notices unusual plant and animal behavior and suspects the seasons are "off"; the learner must identify normal seasonal responses and compare them to what's being observed.
  - Learner evidence: Names at least two specific seasonal responses (one plant, one animal); explains what environmental change triggers each response; connects the response to survival.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.L.17.2

- **Code:** SC.3.L.17.2
- **Strand:** Life Science
- **Standard grouping:** SC.3.L.17 — Interdependence
- **Grade:** 3
- **Official description:** Recognize that plants use energy from the Sun, air, and water to make their own food.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Content complexity level:** Level 1: Recall
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Students understand the basic inputs of photosynthesis at a conceptual level: sunlight (energy), air (carbon dioxide), and water → sugar (food for the plant) + oxygen. At Grade 3, the focus is on the three inputs and the fact that plants are producers. The chemical equation is not expected.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Thinking plants get food from soil (they get minerals/nutrients from soil, but not "food" — they make their own food); thinking fertilizer is plant food; not understanding that plants are the base of most food chains because they produce their own energy.
- **Glossary terms:** Photosynthesis, producer, sunlight, carbon dioxide, water, food (sugar).
- **Vertical alignment:** Critical foundation for understanding food webs and energy flow; connects to L.14.1 (leaf function in food production) and E.5.2 (Sun as energy source).
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - **Access Point In:** SC.3.L.17.In.2 — Recognize that most plants make their own food.
  - **Access Point Su:** SC.3.L.17.Su.2 — Recognize that plants need light to grow.
  - **Access Point Pa:** SC.3.L.17.Pa.2 — Recognize that plants need water.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1651)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner name the three inputs to photosynthesis and explain that plants produce their own food rather than consuming it from the soil?
  - Misconception to detect: Saying plants "eat" soil or fertilizer; not recognizing the Sun as the original energy source for the food the plant makes.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story involving a community garden where someone is debating what "feeds" the plants; the learner corrects a character's misconception and explains photosynthesis at a conceptual level.
  - Learner evidence: Names Sun, air, and water as the three inputs; states that the output is food (sugar) for the plant; correctly explains that soil provides minerals, not food.
