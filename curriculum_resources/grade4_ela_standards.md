## Standard 1: Reading — Reading Prose and Poetry (ELA.4.R.1)

**Strand:** Reading
**Standard Grouping:** Reading Prose and Poetry
**Purpose:** Students read and analyze literary texts with increased sophistication. They examine how multiple story elements collectively drive plot, distinguish between narrator point of view and character perspective, develop themes (stated and implied), and analyze how poetic structure and rhyme create meaning.

---

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.1.1

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.1.1
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.1 — Reading Prose and Poetry
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Explain how setting, events, conflict, and character development contribute to the plot in a literary text.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:** Not explicitly stated on CPALMS page.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) Students move beyond isolated character analysis (Grade 3) to examining how the four major elements — setting, events, conflict, and character development — work together to construct and drive a plot. They explain the contribution of each element, not just list story parts.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: treating plot as a simple sequence of events without connecting how setting creates constraints, conflict creates tension, and character development resolves or deepens that tension; confusing "events" with "plot" as a whole.)
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.1.1: character development alone); leads to Grade 5 analysis of how literary elements interact with craft choices.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Functional Basic Skills in Communications-Elementary (5010030)
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Library Skills/Information Literacy 4 (5011040)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Introduction to Debate Grade 4 (5010104)
  - English for Speakers of Other Languages Grade 4 (5010015)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.1.AP.1 — Show how setting, events, conflict and character development relate to the plot in a literary text.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14957)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify all four elements (setting, events, conflict, character development) in a story passage and explain how each one contributed to a key plot moment?
  - Misconception to detect: Listing elements in isolation ("The setting is a forest; the conflict is a fight") without explaining how they work together; treating plot summary as analysis.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story scene where a character faces a pivotal choice — the learner must explain how the setting, a triggering event, the underlying conflict, and the character's traits all fed into that moment.
  - Learner evidence: A structured explanation (written or oral) naming all four elements with at least one "because" or "which caused" connection showing their relationship.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.1.2

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.1.2
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.1 — Reading Prose and Poetry
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Explain a stated or implied theme and how it develops, using details, in a literary text.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:**
  - Clarification 1: An explanation of how the theme develops should include how characters respond to situations and how the speaker reflects upon a topic in a literary text.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) Students advance beyond Grade 3's stated themes to also identify and explain implied themes — themes the author communicates through character behavior and speaker reflection rather than stating explicitly. Evidence must include how characters' responses to situations and how the speaker's reflections (in poetry or narration) develop the theme over time.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: identifying only stated themes; extracting a theme as a topic word like "friendship" rather than a complete statement; failing to trace theme development across multiple moments in the text.)
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.1.2: stated theme only); leads to Grade 5 analysis of universal theme across multiple texts.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Library Skills/Information Literacy 4 (5011040)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Introduction to Debate Grade 4 (5010104)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.1.AP.2 — Identify a stated theme and how it develops, using details, in a literary text.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14958)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify an implied theme (one not stated directly) and trace its development through at least two character responses and one speaker reflection across the text?
  - Misconception to detect: Restating the plot as the theme; identifying a topic word rather than a thematic statement; only citing the ending rather than tracing development.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer chapter where the central character repeatedly sacrifices personal gain to help others — the theme of "true courage comes from putting others first" is never stated, but emerges through repeated choices; learner must construct the theme and trace it.
  - Learner evidence: A thematic statement expressed as a complete sentence; citations of two or more moments where character behavior or speaker reflection developed it.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.1.3

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.1.3
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.1 — Reading Prose and Poetry
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Identify the narrator's point of view and explain the difference between a narrator's point of view and character perspective in a literary text.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:**
  - Clarification 1: The term perspective means "a particular attitude toward or way of regarding something." The term point of view is used when referring to the person of the narrator. This is to prevent confusion and conflation.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) Students must now do two things simultaneously: (1) identify the technical point of view (first person, third person limited, third person omniscient) and (2) explain how a character's attitude or perspective — which is independent of point of view — differs from or complicates the narrator's presentation. This is a significant step beyond Grade 3's "explain different characters' perspectives."
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: equating narrator's point of view with a character's perspective; assuming first-person narration means the narrator's perspective is always reliable; failing to distinguish between who is telling the story and what any individual character believes.)
- **Glossary terms:** perspective, point of view.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.1.3: character perspectives only); leads to Grade 5 analysis of how point of view influences the telling of events.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Introduction to Debate Grade 4 (5010104)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.1.AP.3 — Identify the narrator's point of view and character perspective in a literary text.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14959)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner correctly name the narrator's point of view (e.g., "third person limited") and explain how a specific character's perspective on an event differs from how the narrator presents it?
  - Misconception to detect: Saying "the narrator's perspective is first person" — confusing grammatical person with attitude; or saying "the narrator and the main character feel the same way" without examining the text.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story told in third-person limited, where the narrator follows a detective but a secondary character believes something entirely different about a mystery — learner must identify the narrative POV and articulate the character's distinct perspective.
  - Learner evidence: Names the point of view correctly; provides a quote or paraphrase from the narrator; contrasts it with a specific character's stated or implied belief.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.1.4

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.1.4
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.1 — Reading Prose and Poetry
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Explain how rhyme and structure create meaning in a poem.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:** Not explicitly stated on CPALMS page.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) Students move beyond identifying poem types (Grade 3) to explaining how the structural and sonic choices the poet made — rhyme scheme, line breaks, stanza groupings, repeated refrains — actively shape the poem's meaning. The question is not just "what type is this?" but "why did the poet structure it this way, and what does that choice do to the meaning?"
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: identifying rhyme scheme without explaining what effect it creates; treating structure as decoration rather than as a meaning-making choice.)
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.1.4: identify poem types); leads to Grade 5 analysis of how figurative language and sound devices affect tone in poetry.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Introduction to Debate Grade 4 (5010104)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.1.AP.4 — Identify repeated words, rhyme or phrases that create meaning in a poem.
- **Supplementary references:**
  - Appendix B (poem form examples) is referenced in the Grade 3 version of this standard and applies here. Grade 4 builds on those form definitions to analyze how structure functions. See `cpalms_supplementary_resources.md`, Resource 1 for Appendix B content.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14960)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify a specific structural element (rhyme scheme, repeated refrain, stanza break) in a poem and explain what meaning or effect that element creates?
  - Misconception to detect: Describing what the poem is about without discussing how structure shapes that meaning; saying "the poem rhymes" without explaining what the rhyme does.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story where a character discovers a poem that contains a hidden clue — learner must analyze how the poet's use of a repeated refrain or a deliberate structural break signals something important about the poem's meaning.
  - Learner evidence: Names a specific structural feature; explains what that feature contributes to the poem's meaning with a quote or paraphrase from the poem as evidence.

## Standard 2: Reading — Reading Informational Text (ELA.4.R.2)

**Strand:** Reading
**Standard Grouping:** Reading Informational Text
**Purpose:** Students read and analyze informational texts with expanded sophistication. They examine new text structures (problem/solution, sequence, description), analyze both implied and explicit central ideas, identify and explain author's perspective, and evaluate the relationship between a claim and its supporting evidence.

---

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.2.1

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.2.1
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.2 — Reading Informational Text
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Explain how text features contribute to the meaning and identify the text structures of problem/solution, sequence, and description in texts.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:** Not explicitly stated on CPALMS page.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) Students now work with three new text structure types (problem/solution, sequence, description) that differ from Grade 3's set (chronology, comparison, cause/effect). They explain how text features (headers, diagrams, timelines, charts, maps) support the meaning created by these structures.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: confusing sequence with chronology; confusing problem/solution with cause/effect; treating "description" as a weak or formless structure rather than recognizing its deliberate organizational pattern.)
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.2.1: chronology, comparison, cause/effect); leads to Grade 5 analysis of how authors use multiple text structures within a single text.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Library Skills/Information Literacy 4 (5011040)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Introduction to Debate Grade 4 (5010104)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.2.AP.1 — Identify the text structures of problem/solution, sequence, description and how they contribute meaning in texts.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14961)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner correctly identify the text structure of a short informational passage, name a specific text feature, and explain how both contribute to the reader's understanding of the content?
  - Misconception to detect: Choosing "cause/effect" or "chronology" (Grade 3 structures) when the text is actually organized as problem/solution; describing text features without connecting them to meaning.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer "mission briefing" scene — learner reads a short document describing a village problem and a proposed solution; must identify the structure and point to one text feature (diagram, subheading) that reinforces the structure.
  - Learner evidence: Correctly names the structure with a signal word from the text; names a text feature and explains what information it adds.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.2.2

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.2.2
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.2 — Reading Informational Text

  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Introduction to Debate Grade 4 (5010104)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.2.AP.2 — Identify relevant details that support an explicit central idea.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14962)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner infer a central idea from a passage where it is not explicitly stated, articulate it as a complete sentence, and identify two or more details that support it?

  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer "field report" where no summary sentence exists — learner reads three paragraphs about an ecosystem and must infer what the author's central idea is, then cite specific supporting details.
  - Learner evidence: Stated implied central idea as a complete sentence; two supporting details identified with brief explanation of why each is relevant (not just interesting).

- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.2 — Reading Informational Text

- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner describe an author's perspective toward a topic and cite specific word choices or details from the text that reveal that perspective?
  - Misconception to detect: Saying the author is "neutral" or "just informing us" without examining word choices; confusing the author's perspective with a character's perspective.

  - Learner evidence: Names the perspective as a specific attitude (e.g., "The author views the dam project as harmful to the environment"); cites two or more word choices or examples that reveal that attitude.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.2.4

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.2.4
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.2 — Reading Informational Text
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Explain an author's claim and the reasons and evidence used to support the claim.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:** Not explicitly stated on CPALMS page.

The emphasis is on explaining the logical structure of the argument.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: conflating a claim with a reason; treating reasons and evidence as the same thing; summarizing the text rather than analyzing the argumentative structure.)
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends ELA.3.R.2.3 and ELA.3.R.2.4; leads to Grade 5 evaluation of whether reasons and evidence are sufficient to support a claim.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Library Skills/Information Literacy 4 (5011040)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.2.AP.4 — Identify an author's claim by selecting evidence and a reason used to support the claim.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14964)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify the claim, name at least two reasons the author gives, and identify the specific evidence used to support one of those reasons?
  - Misconception to detect: Saying "the reason is that the author says X" and citing evidence as the same sentence; treating any detail as evidence without checking whether it actually supports a reason.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer "council debate" scene where a character presents a written argument — learner must identify the claim, map out the reason-evidence structure, and explain how one piece of evidence connects back to the claim.
  - Learner evidence: Correctly identifies claim, two reasons, and at least one piece of evidence; explains the logical connection between evidence and claim.

## Standard 3: Reading — Reading Across Genres (ELA.4.R.3)

**Strand:** Reading
**Standard Grouping:** Reading Across Genres
**Purpose:** Students apply reading skills across genres. They explain how figurative language (expanded from Grade 3) contributes to meaning, summarize across text types, and — new at Grade 4 — compare and contrast primary and secondary source accounts of the same event.

---

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.3.1

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.3.1
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.3 — Reading Across Genres
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Explain how figurative language contributes to meaning in text(s).
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:**
  - Clarification 1: Figurative language for the purposes of this benchmark refers to metaphor, simile, alliteration, personification, hyperbole, and idiom. Other examples can be used in instruction.
  - Clarification 2: See Elementary Figurative Language.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) Grade 4 shifts from identifying figurative language (Grade 3: "identify and explain") to explaining how it *contributes to meaning* — the emphasis is on function, not just recognition. Students explain what effect a specific device creates on the reader's understanding of the text. All six device types are now active targets.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: identifying the device without explaining its contribution; saying "this is a metaphor" without explaining what it helps the reader understand; treating all figurative language as decorative rather than functional.)
- **Glossary terms:** metaphor, simile, alliteration, personification, hyperbole, idiom.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.3.1: identify and explain metaphors, personification, hyperbole while continuing simile/alliteration/idiom); leads to Grade 5 analysis of how figurative language affects tone.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.3.AP.1 — Identify examples of when figurative language is used to contribute to meaning in text(s).
- **Supplementary references:**
  - **Elementary Figurative Language (Appendix D):** Referenced directly by this benchmark. Defines all six device types with examples. Grade 4 expands from Grade 3's three new devices to all six being fully active. See `cpalms_supplementary_resources.md`, Resource 5 for content summary.
  - Source URL: https://cpalmsmediaprod.blob.core.windows.net/uploads/docs/standards/best/la/appendixd.pdf
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14965)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify a specific figurative device in a story passage, name it correctly, and explain what it contributes to the reader's understanding of the character, setting, or theme?
  - Misconception to detect: Restating the figurative expression in plain language without explaining why the author used it; identifying the device type but not its meaning-making function.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer story passage where a character facing danger is described through personification ("the fog crept in and swallowed the lights one by one") — learner identifies the device and explains how it makes the setting feel threatening in a way a literal description wouldn't.
  - Learner evidence: Names the figurative device; explains in one to two sentences what the device contributes to the meaning that a literal phrase would not.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.3.2

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.3.2
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.3 — Reading Across Genres
- **Grade:** 4

The sophistication lies in the quality and brevity of the summary, not just coverage of required elements.

- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Mirrors Grade 3 (ELA.3.R.3.2) in structure; Grade 4 applies higher text complexity and adds implied central idea from R.2.2.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Library Skills/Information Literacy 4 (5011040)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - **Access Point (literary):** ELA.4.R.3.AP.2a — Identify the plot and theme for a literary text using the student's mode of communication.
  - **Access Point (informational):** ELA.4.R.3.AP.2b — Identify the central idea and relevant details for an informational text using the student's mode of communication.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14966)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner produce a 3–5 sentence summary of a Grade 4 literary passage (including theme) or informational passage (including implied central idea) that is concise, objective, and covers essential content?
  - Misconception to detect: Summary that is as long as the original; summary with no theme or central idea; summary that includes the learner's opinion about the text.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: After each Primer chapter, learner writes a "case summary" as a character — for literary chapters it includes plot arc + theme; for informational inserts it includes the implied central idea + key evidence.

### Benchmark: ELA.4.R.3.3

- **Code:** ELA.4.R.3.3
- **Strand:** Reading
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.R.3 — Reading Across Genres
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Compare and contrast accounts of the same event using primary and/or secondary sources.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:**
  - Clarification 1: Introduce the terms primary sources and secondary sources.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) New at Grade 4 — students learn to distinguish between primary sources (created by someone who directly witnessed or participated in an event: diaries, letters, photographs, speeches, original documents) and secondary sources (created after the fact using primary sources: textbooks, encyclopedias, biographies). They then compare two accounts of the same event across source types and explain key similarities and differences.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: categorizing a newspaper report as a primary source when it is secondary; identifying only surface-level content differences rather than explaining why the two accounts differ — e.g., because of perspective or purpose.)
- **Glossary terms:** primary source, secondary source.
- **Vertical alignment:** New benchmark at Grade 4; leads to Grade 5 work with synthesizing information across multiple sources and evaluating source reliability.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.R.3.AP.3 — Compare a primary and secondary source on the same event.
- **Supplementary references:** None directly cited on CPALMS page beyond the definition note. Primer should introduce these definitions explicitly when setting up the scenario.

## Standard 10: Foundational Skills — Learning and Applying Foundational Reading Skills (ELA.4.F.1)

**Strand:** Foundational Skills
**Standard Grouping:** Learning and Applying Foundational Reading Skills
**Purpose:** Grade 4 students apply advanced phonics through syllabication and morpheme-level decoding (a pedagogical upgrade from phoneme-level decoding) and read grade-level texts with the accuracy, automaticity, and prosody appropriate for the Lexile band 740L–1010L. (Note: ELA.4.F.1.1 and .1.2 are not present at Grade 4; those benchmarks address print concepts and phonological awareness, which are mastered by the end of Grade 2 in the B.E.S.T. progression.)

### Benchmark: ELA.4.F.1.3

- **Code:** ELA.4.F.1.3
- **Strand:** Foundational Skills
- **Standard grouping:** ELA.4.F.1 — Learning and Applying Foundational Reading Skills
- **Grade:** 4
- **Official description:** Use knowledge of grade-level phonics and word-analysis skills to decode words. Apply knowledge of all letter-sound correspondences, syllabication patterns, and morphology to read and write unfamiliar single-syllable and multisyllabic words in and out of context.
- **Official examples:** None explicitly stated.
- **Clarifications:**
  - Clarification 1: At this level of reading, a student who is decoding at the phoneme level (i.e., "e-n-t-er-t-ai-n") may decode a given text, but will struggle with fluency and comprehension. As such, phonics instruction should move toward decoding at the syllabication and morpheme level. For example, when a 4th grader encounters the word "entertain" in text, we want him or her to segment by syllable (i.e., "en-ter-tain") or by morphological structure (i.e., "enter-tain").
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** (Inferred) This benchmark marks a critical pedagogical shift — from phoneme-level decoding (sounding out individual letters/sounds) to syllabication and morpheme-level decoding (processing meaningful chunks). The clarification gives the specific example of *entertain*: the Grade 4 expected approach is *en-ter-tain* (syllables) or *enter-tain* (morphemes), not *e-n-t-e-r-t-a-i-n* (phonemes). This directly enables fluency.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: continuing to decode letter-by-letter even for known word parts; not recognizing morpheme boundaries; not connecting F.1.3 to V.1.2 — the same morphological knowledge supports both decoding and vocabulary.)
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends Grade 3 (ELA.3.F.1.3: Greek/Latin roots/affixes, derivational suffixes, multisyllabic words); Grade 4 explicitly requires syllabication and morpheme-level processing as the expected strategy.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Language Arts - Grade Four (5010045)
  - Access Language Arts - Grade 4 (7710015)
  - Basic Skills in Reading 3–5 (5010024)
  - Functional Reading Skills 3-5 (5010026)
  - **Access Point:** ELA.4.F.1.AP.3a — Apply knowledge of letter-sound correspondences, syllabication patterns, and morphology to read and form familiar single-syllable and multisyllabic words in context.
- **Supplementary references:**
  - **Common Greek and Latin Roots 3–5 and Affixes:** Referenced by the standard for both decoding and vocabulary. Students apply the same root/affix knowledge for word reading (F.1.3) and word meaning (V.1.2). See `cpalms_supplementary_resources.md`, Resources 7 and 8.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/14955)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: When a Primer story contains a highlighted multisyllabic word, can the learner segment it by syllables or morphemes (not letter-by-letter) and use that structure to decode and define it?
  - Misconception to detect: Letter-by-letter decoding of *entertain* instead of syllable-level (*en-ter-tain*) or morpheme-level (*enter-tain*); not recognizing that this is the same skill as vocabulary root analysis in V.1.2.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: Primer "word unlock" mechanic — learner encounters a highlighted word (e.g., *subterranean*, *entertainment*, *discoverability*) and taps to segment it at the syllable or morpheme level before the story continues.
  - Learner evidence: Correct syllabic or morphemic segmentation (not phonemic); accurate identification of the word's meaning from its parts; correct pronunciation of the assembled word.
