## Big Idea 1: The Practice of Science (SC.3.N.1)

**Body of Knowledge:** Nature of Science
**Big Idea Summary:**
- A: Scientific inquiry is a multifaceted activity; the processes of science include the formulation of scientifically investigable questions, construction of investigations into those questions, the collection of appropriate data, the evaluation of the meaning of those data, and the communication of this evaluation.
- B: The processes of science frequently do not correspond to the traditional portrayal of "the scientific method."
- C: Scientific argumentation is a necessary part of scientific inquiry and plays an important role in the generation and validation of scientific knowledge.
- D: Scientific knowledge is based on observation and inference; it is important to recognize that these are very different things. Not only does science require creativity in its methods and processes, but also in its questions and explanations.

**CPALMS Course:** Science - Grade Three (5020040)

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.1

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.1
- **Body of Knowledge:** Nature of Science
- **Big Idea:** The Practice of Science (Big Idea 1)
- **Grade:** 3
- **Content Complexity:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Official description:** Raise questions about the natural world, investigate them individually and in teams through free exploration and systematic investigations, and generate appropriate explanations based on those explorations.
- **Official examples:** (No explicitly labeled "examples" on CPALMS; the following are from associated instructional resources on CPALMS.)
  - Physical Science Unit: Water Beach Vacation — students raise questions about evaporation, condensation, and melting through a series of investigation lessons.
  - Response to the Cues — students design and test a plant terrarium by asking questions, planning, creating, and testing.
  - Teaching the Scientific Method Through Thumb Wars — students form a hypothesis, run multiple trials, record data, and draw conclusions.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Not explicitly stated on CPALMS page.
  - (Inferred) Students practice the full inquiry cycle: questioning → exploring → investigating → explaining. Both free exploration and systematic investigations are valued; neither alone is sufficient. Explanations should be grounded in what was observed.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated.
  - (Inferred) Students may think science always follows a rigid step-by-step "scientific method" (Big Idea element B directly counters this); may confuse observation (what you see) with inference (what you conclude).
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - Library Skills/Information Literacy Grade 3 (5011030)
  - STEM Lab Grade 3 (5020100)
  - **Access Points:**
    - SC.3.N.1.In.1 — Ask questions, explore, observe, and identify outcomes.
    - SC.3.N.1.Su.1 — Ask literal questions, explore, observe, and share information.
    - SC.3.N.1.Pa.1 — Explore, observe, and recognize common objects in the natural world.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified — official description confirmed from CPALMS standards document and CPALMS search (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/Preview/1626); access points confirmed from print page.
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner generate an investigable question, plan a way to test it, record observations, and form an explanation grounded in what they found?
  - Misconception to detect: Treating a conclusion as a fact before evidence is gathered; confusing "I think" (inference) with "I saw" (observation); expecting a fixed step-by-step method.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer investigation arc — something unexplained is happening in a story world (a plant is growing differently, an animal is behaving strangely); learner is asked to form a question, propose a test, interpret results, and generate an explanation.
  - Learner evidence: Articulates a testable question; records observations distinctly from conclusions; produces an explanation that references evidence gathered.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.1.2

- **Code:** SC.3.N.1.2
- **Body of Knowledge:** Nature of Science
- **Big Idea:** The Practice of Science (Big Idea 1)
- **Grade:** 3
- **Content Complexity:** Level 3: Strategic Thinking & Complex Reasoning
- **Official description:** Compare the observations made by different groups using the same tools and seek reasons to explain the differences across groups.
- **Official examples:** (Inferred from associated resources.)
  - Physical Science Unit: Water Beach Vacation Lesson 1 Observations — multiple groups observe properties of water using the same tools and compare results.
  - Measuring Matters! — students generate measurement data using rulers and compare results.
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Not explicitly stated.
  - (Inferred) Students recognize that different groups can produce different observations even using the same tools; they practice seeking explanations for variation (differences in measurement technique, observer error, natural variation) rather than assuming one group is simply wrong.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated.
  - (Inferred) Students may assume that any difference between group results means one group made an error; may not consider that natural variation or technique differences can explain discrepancies.
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - STEM Lab Grade 3 (5020100)
  - **Access Points:**
    - SC.3.N.1.In.2 — Work with a group to make observations and identify results.
    - SC.3.N.1.Su.2 — Work with a partner to make observations.
    - SC.3.N.1.Pa.2 — Assist with investigations with a partner.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified — description confirmed from CPALMS standards document and search (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/Preview/1627); access points confirmed from print page.
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner compare their observations to another group's and identify differences; can they propose at least one reason why results might differ?
  - Misconception to detect: Automatically declaring a different result as "wrong" rather than exploring why results may differ.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer collaborative investigation scene — two explorer teams measured the same thing in a story world but got different readings; learner must compare and reason about why.
  - Learner evidence: Identifies a specific difference between two sets of observations; proposes a plausible explanation that does not simply blame error.

## Big Idea 3: The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models (SC.3.N.3)

**Body of Knowledge:** Nature of Science
**Big Idea Summary:** The terms that describe examples of scientific knowledge — "theory," "law," "hypothesis," and "model" — have very specific meanings and functions within science that differ from their everyday usage.

---

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.3.1

- **Code:** SC.3.N.3.1
- **Body of Knowledge:** Nature of Science
- **Big Idea:** The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models (Big Idea 3)
- **Grade:** 3
- **Content Complexity:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Status:** State Board Approved
- **Official description:** Recognize that words in science can have different or more specific meanings than their use in everyday language; for example, energy, cell, heat/cold, and evidence.
- **Official examples:** (From CPALMS page.)
  - energy, cell, heat/cold, evidence (named in the benchmark itself as illustrations).
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Not explicitly stated on CPALMS page.
  - (Inferred) Students learn that scientific vocabulary is precise and may conflict with or differ from everyday uses of the same words. This is a metacognitive language benchmark as much as a content benchmark.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated.
  - (Inferred) Students may apply everyday meanings to scientific terms (e.g., "energy" as something you get from food/sleep vs. the physics concept; "cell" as a phone cell vs. a biological cell; "cold" as a thing rather than an absence of heat energy).
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - STEM Lab Grade 3 (5020100)
  - **Access Points:**
    - SC.3.N.3.In.1 — Recognize meanings of words used in science, such as energy, temperature, and gravity.
    - SC.3.N.3.Su.1 — Recognize meanings of words used in science, such as telescope, environment, and solid.
    - SC.3.N.3.Pa.1 — Recognize common objects related to science by name, such as ice, animal, and plant.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1636)
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify when a word is being used in a scientific sense and explain how its scientific meaning differs from everyday use?
  - Misconception to detect: Applying colloquial meanings of "theory" (just a guess), "energy" (general vitality), or "evidence" (anything said by someone) in a scientific context.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer vocabulary discovery scene — a character uses a scientific word the learner misunderstands; the story pauses to examine the word's scientific meaning vs. everyday meaning.
  - Learner evidence: Can explain why a scientific word means something different in context; provides both the everyday and scientific meanings.

### Benchmark: SC.3.N.3.3

- **Code:** SC.3.N.3.3
- **Body of Knowledge:** Nature of Science
- **Big Idea:** The Role of Theories, Laws, Hypotheses, and Models (Big Idea 3)
- **Grade:** 3
- **Content Complexity:** Level 2: Basic Application of Skills & Concepts
- **Status:** State Board Approved
- **Official description:** Recognize that all models are approximations of natural phenomena; as such, they do not perfectly account for all observations.
- **Official examples:** Not explicitly stated. (Inferred: a model of the water cycle shows evaporation and condensation but cannot capture every detail of a specific local weather event.)
- **Clarifications:** None explicitly stated.
- **Purpose and instructional strategies:** Not explicitly stated.
  - (Inferred) Students build on SC.3.N.3.2 by recognizing that models always leave some things out or simplify in ways that mean they will not match every observation perfectly. This is a normal and accepted part of science, not a flaw.
- **Common misconceptions or errors:** Not explicitly stated.
  - (Inferred) Students may reject a model because it does not match one specific observation, not recognizing that approximation is expected; may think a "broken" model is useless.
- **Glossary terms:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Vertical alignment:** Extends SC.3.N.3.2.
- **Related courses or access points:**
  - Science - Grade Three (5020040)
  - Access Science Grade 3 (7720040)
  - STEM Lab Grade 3 (5020100)
  - **Access Points:**
    - SC.3.N.3.Su.2 — Recognize that models represent real things.
    - SC.3.N.3.Pa.2 — Recognize a model of a real object.
    - SC.3.N.3.In.3 — Identify that models are representations of things found in the real world.
- **Verification status:** Fully verified — description confirmed from CPALMS search snippet and print page (https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/Preview/1638).
- **Primer incorporation notes:**
  - Skill observed: Can the learner identify a limitation of a model and explain why the model is still useful despite that limitation?
  - Misconception to detect: Dismissing a model entirely because it doesn't explain one observation; thinking a perfect model is possible.
  - Narrative/problem contexts: A Primer scene where a character uses a weather model that predicted rain, but it didn't rain; learner reasons about why the model was still useful and what it couldn't account for.
  - Learner evidence: Names a specific limitation of a model; explains why the model remains valuable even with that limitation.
